The Wilcox Bas-Relief & The March Delirium

In the spring of 1925, a neurotic young Rhode Island School of Design sculptor named Henry Anthony Wilcox presented Professor George Gammell Angell with a damp clay bas-relief. The sculpture depicted a crouching, tentacled monstrosity crowned with rudimentary wings, surrounded by characters of an unknown pictographic tongue.

Wilcox had sculpted it in a hypnagogic frenzy during a night pierced by seismic oceanic tremors. Simultaneously, across London, Paris, and Manila, sensitive painters, poets, and dreamers plummeted into acute delirium, speaking of cyclopean cities of dripping green slime.

“It seemed to be a sort of monster, or symbol representing a monster, of a form which only a diseased fancy could conceive... A pulpy, tentacled head surmounted a grotesque and scaly body with rudimentary wings.”

Wax facsimile of Wilcox's bas-relief. Note the squid-like cephalopod cranial structure and tripartite ocular arrangement.

The Louisiana Bayou Raid (1907) & The Aklo Chant

Eighteen years before Wilcox's fever, New Orleans police inspector John Raymond Legrasse raided an unholy nocturnal orgy in the impassable swamps south of New Orleans. Savages and half-castes danced around a mound topped by an identical greenish-black stone statuette.

Under interrogation, the octogenarian mestizo Castro revealed the truth: the Great Old Ones were not native to Earth. They filtered down from the stars when the world was young, and now lie dormant inside their sunken stone citadel, preserved by the spells of their High Priest.

The Log of Gustaf Johansen & Point Nemo

On March 23, 1925, the two-masted schooner Emma encountered the cultist vessel Alert in the remote South Pacific. Following a deadly skirmish, second mate Gustaf Johansen sailed the captured ship to charted coordinates:

📍 47° 9′ S, 126° 43′ W — The Oceanic Pole of Inaccessibility.

There, rising above the waves, stood the nightmare corpse-city of R'lyeh. Johansen described cyclopean masonry of slimy green basalt, vast monoliths where angles were acute yet behaved as obtuse, and a catastrophic portal sealed by a gargantuan carved lintel.

When the seal gave way, the Thing burst forth. Johansen was the sole survivor, saving his vessel only by ramming the steel prow of the Alert straight into the nebulous titan's gelatinous head.

The Ramming of the Alert

“There was a mighty eddying and churning in the water, and Johansen drove the vessel straight at the pulpy monster. The awful squid-head yielded with a burst as of a bursting sunfish... but even as the steamer plowed on, the scattered jelly reconstituted, grim and immortal.”

Johansen died mysteriously in Oslo in 1926 shortly after penning this manuscript.

The Physics of Impossible Angles

Lovecraft repeatedly noted that R'lyeh was built according to non-Euclidean geometry. In terrestrial 3-space, the interior angles of a triangle always sum to 180°, and parallel lines never converge.

Inside R'lyeh, space curvature is hyper-Riemannian or projected from a fourth spatial dimension. A human observing a wall perceives a plane surface, yet stepping upon it causes them to slip sideways as gravity re-orientates toward an unseen focal point.

“He had said that the geometry of the dream-place he saw was abnormal, non-Euclidean, and loathsomely redolent of spheres and dimensions apart from ours.”

Cosmic Indifferentism

Traditional religion and Gothic fiction presume the universe cares about humanity—whether as beloved children or prey. In Lovecraft’s cosmos, there is neither benevolence nor malevolence. Humanity is an ephemeral biological fungus on a dying rock. Cthulhu’s waking spells our doom not from malice, but sheer atmospheric displacement.

The Priest, Not the Sovereign

Popular culture misidentifies Cthulhu as the supreme godhead. Within the true mythos, Cthulhu is merely the High Priest. Far above him lie the blind, mindless Outer Gods: Azathoth, boiling in nuclear chaos at the center of infinity, and Yog-Sothoth, the coterminous continuum. Cthulhu himself bows to forces beyond our comprehension.
